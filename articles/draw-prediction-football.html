Why Draws Are Hard to Predict

The draw is the outcome that models often treat as a leftover. It deserves its own attention because it is neither rare nor easy.

By Nyasha Published 10 October 2026 Last updated 10 October 2026 8 min read

Most pre-match discussion focuses on who will win. The draw is treated as the absence of a result, a dull outcome somewhere between the two teams. Yet draws are common enough that ignoring them is a serious analytical mistake.

In many league seasons, roughly one match in four ends level. The exact rate varies by competition, era and team style, but the draw is never a rounding error. It is a distinct outcome with its own probability, and modelling it well requires more care than subtracting the home and away chances from 100 per cent.

Key takeaways

  • Draws occur in roughly a quarter of league matches, with variation by competition and season.
  • Independent Poisson models often misprice low-score draws and can be adjusted.
  • Game state changes the probability of a draw as the match progresses.
  • Draw estimates are sensitive to the expected goal rates and the dependence assumptions.
  • A draw probability is a description of uncertainty, not a prediction of a specific result.

Start with the base rate

The first useful number is the league's draw rate. A competition that produces draws in 26 per cent of matches gives you a baseline. A competition with a 22 per cent rate gives you a different one. The baseline is not a prediction for a specific match, but it is a sensible starting point.

Draw rates vary for structural reasons. Leagues with greater parity may produce more draws because teams are more evenly matched. Leagues with a wider gap between strong and weak teams may produce fewer, as the stronger side is more likely to win. Styles matter too: two cautious teams can cancel each other out, while two open teams may trade goals.

The base rate also varies over time. Rule changes, scheduling, and tactical trends can shift the balance. A model that uses a decade-old draw rate without checking recent seasons may be starting from the wrong place.

Why Poisson models struggle with draws

A basic Poisson model treats each team's goals as independent. The probability of a 0-0 draw is the probability that the home team scores zero multiplied by the probability that the away team scores zero. The same logic applies to 1-1, 2-2 and so on.

Independence is a convenient assumption, but it is not fully true. The score affects behaviour. A team that falls behind may attack more, which raises the chance of both scoring and conceding. A team that leads may defend deeper, which lowers the chance of an equaliser but also lowers its own scoring rate. These effects can change the shape of the scoreline distribution, especially around low scores.

Real football often produces more 0-0 and 1-0 results than independent Poisson predicts, and fewer high-scoring draws. The Dixon-Coles adjustment is one way to correct the low-score cells. It adds a dependence parameter that nudges the probabilities towards observed patterns. The adjustment is not a magic fix, but it is a recognition that the simple model is incomplete.

Game state and the chance of a draw

The probability of a draw is not constant during a match. Before kick-off, it is a pre-match estimate. After ten minutes, it depends on the score, the sendings off, the tactical approach and the time remaining.

A 0-0 score at half-time raises the conditional probability of a draw compared with the pre-match estimate, because there are fewer minutes left for a goal. A 2-2 score at half-time may also raise it, but for a different reason: the teams have shown they can score, and another goal is plausible. A 3-0 score makes the draw very unlikely.

This is why in-play probabilities require a different model from pre-match probabilities. The pre-match model describes the distribution before the match begins. Once the score changes, the relevant question changes. Using the pre-match draw probability after a goal is a common error.

A worked example

The example below is illustrative. Club A has an expected goal rate of 1.55 and Club B has 1.05. We compare a simple independent Poisson estimate with a Dixon-Coles style adjustment that raises the 0-0 and 1-1 cells and lowers the 2-2 cell.

Illustrative draw probabilities
ScoreIndependent PoissonAdjusted model
0-06.78%7.60%
1-111.05%11.70%
2-24.50%4.10%
3-31.22%1.10%
Total draw24.15%24.90%

The independent model gives a draw probability of about 24.2 per cent. The adjusted model gives about 24.9 per cent. The totals are close, but the composition differs. The adjustment moves probability towards the low-score draws and away from the high-score ones.

Now change the expected goal rates. If Club A's rate rises from 1.55 to 1.85 and Club B's stays at 1.05, the draw probability falls. The stronger team is more likely to win, and the chance of a level score drops. The exact figure depends on the model, but the direction is consistent: higher expected goal differences tend to reduce draw probability.

The example also shows why a draw estimate should not be treated as a single fixed number. A small change in the inputs can move the probability by a percentage point or two. The model is a structured way to think about the outcome, not an oracle.

Why draws are often misread

The draw is easy to overlook because it lacks a hero. A win has a scorer and a story; a draw has a moment of frustration or a defensive stand. That narrative bias can affect analysis. Teams may be described as "settling for a point" when the draw was the most likely outcome all along.

There is also a market angle. Draws are often priced at longer odds than the two win outcomes, which can make them look either attractive or irrelevant depending on the observer. The price is not a prediction. It reflects the market's estimate of the probability, the margin, and the flow of money. Our guide to implied probability explains how to convert prices into probabilities before comparing them with a model.

Another error is to assume that a draw is more likely when two teams are evenly matched. That is often true, but not always. Two evenly matched attacking teams may produce more goals and fewer draws than two evenly matched defensive teams. The expected goal rates matter as much as the gap between them.

League and style effects

Draw rates differ across competitions. A league with a compact table and many similar teams may produce more level results. A league with a dominant top group may produce fewer. Cup competitions add another layer because extra time and penalties change the definition of a draw.

Team style also matters. A low-block team that concedes possession may draw more often than its shot numbers suggest, because it keeps matches close. A high-pressing team may create more variance: it can win comfortably or lose by a large margin. The draw probability is a product of the whole distribution, not just the average quality of the two sides.

Common mistakes

  • Subtracting win probabilities from 100 per cent and assigning the remainder to the draw. The draw has its own probability and should be modelled directly.
  • Using independent Poisson without checking the low-score cells. The simple model can misstate 0-0 and 1-1.
  • Assuming evenly matched teams always draw more. The goal rates and styles matter as much as the quality gap.
  • Applying a pre-match draw probability after a goal. The conditional probability changes with the score and the time remaining.
  • Treating a draw estimate as a certainty. A 25 per cent chance means the draw is more likely to not happen than to happen.

These mistakes usually come from treating the draw as a residual category. Once it is modelled as an outcome in its own right, the analysis becomes clearer.

Limits and caveats

No pre-match model knows the lineup, the referee, the weather or the mood of the players. The draw probability is an estimate conditional on the inputs, and the inputs are uncertain. A model that reports 25 per cent is not claiming that the match will be level one time in four. It is claiming that, across many similar matches, about a quarter would end level if the model were well calibrated.

Calibration is the test. A model that assigns 25 per cent to draws should see draws in roughly 25 per cent of those cases over a large sample. That is a different question from whether the model picked the right result in a single match. For more on evaluating probabilities rather than just winners, see our guide to tracking prediction accuracy.

The draw is hard to predict because football is hard to predict. The honest approach is to estimate it explicitly, state the uncertainty, and avoid the temptation to treat a level score as either a failure or a moral victory.

Frequently asked questions

What percentage of football matches end in a draw?

It varies by competition and season, but many league competitions produce draws in roughly 22 to 28 per cent of matches. The base rate is a useful starting point, not a fixed law.

Why does a Poisson model underestimate some draws?

The basic model assumes independent goal rates and a constant rate through the match. Real football has score effects and dependence between the teams, which can change the low-score probabilities.

Is a draw more likely when two teams are evenly matched?

Often, but not automatically. The expected goal rates, the styles and the game state matter. Two evenly matched attacking teams can produce fewer draws than two defensive teams with a similar quality gap.
