Crossing and Cutbacks: Choosing the Better Ball

A cross and a cutback both arrive from wide areas, but they ask different questions of the defence. The better ball is the one that fits the runners, the space and the goalkeeper.

By Nyasha Published 25 November 2026 Last updated 25 November 2026 9 min read

Wide areas produce two common deliveries: the cross from a deeper or wider position, and the cutback from near the byline. Both are passes into the penalty area, yet they ask different questions of the defence and the attackers in the box.

Commentary often treats them as one category. A hopeful ball from 30 yards and a cutback rolled into the path of a late runner are not the same chance. Value depends on where the ball starts, how fast it travels, and whether anyone is moving to meet it.

This guide explains the difference, the delivery types, how defenders and goalkeepers read them, and how to judge quality beyond the scoreline.

Key takeaways

  • A cross is delivered from a wide position towards the box, while a cutback is played back from near the byline into the path of a runner.
  • Delivery height, pace and direction decide who can attack the ball and who can deal with it.
  • Defenders read the flight and the runner; goalkeepers read the trajectory, the space and their starting position.
  • A cutback often creates a better angle, but it needs a runner arriving and a team able to reach the byline.
  • Judge delivery with location, completion, shot quality and repeatability rather than goals alone.

Crosses and cutbacks are different passes

A cross is usually struck from a wide channel, at or beyond the edge of the penalty area, and directed towards the central space in front of goal. The attacker meeting it is often moving towards goal and may have to head the ball or adjust their feet to shoot.

A cutback, sometimes called a pull-back, starts closer to the byline. The player carries the ball towards the goal line and then passes backwards, away from goal, towards the penalty spot or the edge of the six-yard box. The ball travels into the path of a teammate arriving from midfield or the opposite flank.

The geometry changes the defending. A defender facing a cross can usually see the ball and the attacker at the same time. A defender facing a cutback may have to turn, because the pass moves into the space behind or beside them. That makes cutbacks dangerous when they work, but not automatically the better ball. A cutback needs a carrier who can reach the byline, a runner who times the arrival, and space to make the pass.

The main delivery types

The early cross comes from deeper, often before the defence has set. It asks a forward to attack the space behind the back line, but gives the attacker less time to adjust. The whipped cross is flat and fast, curled between goalkeeper and defenders, asking for a glancing touch or a run across the near post.

The floated cross is slower and higher, giving the goalkeeper time to claim and defenders time to organise. It can still work when the attacking team has a clear aerial advantage, but it depends on the first contact. The low driven cross travels hard along the ground towards the six-yard box, needing a runner at the far post or a striker attacking the near post.

The cutback is pulled back from the byline, usually along the ground. It targets a runner arriving late, often a midfielder or the opposite winger. The shot that follows is usually taken facing goal, with the goalkeeper moving across and defenders turning. That is why cutbacks are linked with high-value chances, although the pass still has to beat the defenders in the lane.

Delivery choice should follow the runners. A team with a tall target forward may value the floated or whipped cross, while a team with late-arriving midfielders may prefer the cutback. Our guide to build-up play explains how teams create the territory for these deliveries.

How defenders and goalkeepers read the ball

Defending a delivery starts before the ball is struck. The defender checks the position of the carrier, the body shape, the runners and the space around the penalty spot. The first job is to protect the most dangerous space, not the nearest opponent.

On a cross, defenders often divide responsibility between the near post, the central zone and the back post. The central defenders track the main attackers and attack the ball. A cutback is a different read. The defender cannot face the ball and the runner at the same time, so the best defenders watch the carrier's hips and step into the lane before the pass is played.

The goalkeeper's reading is central. On a cross, the keeper judges the trajectory, the crowd in front of goal and the distance needed to claim. A keeper too deep gives attackers room to head; too far forward risks a ball lifted over them. On a cutback, the keeper cannot come and claim because the ball is moving away from goal. The priority shifts to setting for the shot while the defence turns. A keeper who claims crosses changes the value of a floated delivery, so the same ball can suit one keeper and not another.

A worked example

The table below is illustrative. It compares two teams' wide delivery profiles over six matches. The numbers are invented for teaching.

Team A delivered far more often but generated less than half of Team B's expected goals from wide play. The cross count suggests intent and territory. The completion and shot numbers suggest the deliveries rarely found a teammate in a dangerous position.

Team B took the opposite route. It reached the byline more often, completed most of its cutbacks, and produced six shots per match from those passes. The profile fits a team with a late runner and a carrier able to reach the goal line. It does not prove cutbacks are always better; Team B may simply have had the players and space to execute them.

Now imagine the goals had read 0 for Team B and 2 for Team A. The expected goals would still favour Team B's process. A deflected cross can swing a result without changing which team created the better deliveries. Game state also matters: Team A may have been chasing matches and crossing more often against a set defence.

Judging delivery quality without overreading goals

A useful assessment separates four layers. Volume is how often the team attempts a delivery and from where. Execution is how often the ball reaches a teammate or a dangerous zone. Reception is whether the delivery produces a shot. Quality is the expected goals value of the chances created.

Each layer answers a different question. A team may attempt many crosses because it is behind and the opponent is defending deep. That describes the game state as much as the crossing. A team may complete few crosses but create good chances because one delivery found the right runner. Completion alone can reward safe balls that never trouble the defence.

Compare the numbers with the team's own baseline and the opponent's defensive profile. Crossing against a low block is a different task from crossing against a high line. Our article on the low block explains how defenders protect the central space while conceding wide areas. Then watch the sequences. Did the cross follow a switch, and was the box loaded with runners?

Goals are a poor primary measure because they are rare. A single cross can produce a goal from a deflection, a defensive mistake or a moment of individual quality that has little to do with the delivery. Our guide to expected threat offers another way to value the possession that leads to a delivery, before the shot is taken.

Common mistakes

  • Counting crosses as chances. A cross is a pass, not a shot. Twenty deliveries that never find a teammate are not twenty chances.
  • Treating completion as quality. A safe ball back to the edge of the box may be completed without threatening the goal.
  • Ignoring the runners. A delivery can be well struck and still fail because nobody attacked the near post.
  • Judging from one goal or one assist. A deflection can make a poor delivery look decisive. Look at the pattern across several matches.
  • Forgetting the game state. Trailing teams cross more often, so raw counts partly measure the scoreline rather than the plan.
  • Assuming a cutback is always better. It needs a carrier who can reach the byline and a runner who times the arrival.

The common thread is the difference between a delivery and a chance. A delivery moves the ball into the box; a chance is the shot that follows.

Using delivery data sensibly

Delivery data has clear limits. Event records usually capture the start and end point of a pass, but not the pace, the spin or the defender's touch. A completed cross may have been intended for one runner and deflected to another. A cutback may be recorded as incomplete when a defender intercepted it. Definitions also differ between providers, so compare like with like.

Video is the necessary partner. It shows whether the delivery was the right choice, whether the box was loaded, and how the goalkeeper and defenders reacted. Used with care, this analysis shows where the final ball comes from, who is meant to meet it, and whether the defence is being asked a difficult question.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a cross and a cutback?

A cross is delivered from a wide area towards the central space in front of goal. A cutback is played backwards from near the byline towards the penalty spot, aiming for a late runner.

Is a cutback always a better chance than a cross?

No. Cutbacks often give the shooter a better angle and force defenders to turn, but they require a player to reach the byline and a runner to arrive at the right time.

How should I judge crossing quality without using goals?

Look at the number of deliveries, where they start, how many reach a teammate or a dangerous zone, and the expected goals value of the shots they produce. Then watch whether the runners matched the delivery.
